Summer on the
Cape Fear.

Tides first, weather second. What to know about planning a summer trip to Shark Tooth Island — and how to make the most of the season.

Summer is peak season on the North Carolina coast, and Shark Tooth Island rewards the same planning discipline that works the rest of the year — just applied a little more deliberately. The formula is the same as always: pick your tide window first, then check weather. Summer just makes both decisions slightly more important.

The good news is that summer low tides tend to fall in the morning, which is when you want to be out there anyway. Early starts and good tide timing is most of the battle.

The island has shade — the interior has tree cover worth using. Where you’ll be hunting, the shoreline is open. Build breaks into the plan.

Planning Around the Tide

Low tide is the primary variable for a productive trip, and in summer it often lands between 6 and 10 AM — which aligns well with getting out before the day heats up. The Hunt Planner shows the next seven days of tide windows and weather scoring together, so you can find where the two line up. New and full moon weeks produce the largest tidal ranges and expose the most shoreline — worth planning around when you can.

The Weather Pattern

Summer afternoons on the Cape Fear coast follow a familiar convective cycle. Mornings are typically clear. By late morning, cumulus starts building inland. By midday you have your read on how the afternoon is going to go. Most days are fine. Some days aren’t.

The practical approach: check the forecast the morning of, note what time cumulus usually starts building for that day’s setup, and plan your return accordingly. A morning low tide with a clear forecast is an easy call. If clouds are stacking by noon while you’re still on the island, that’s your cue to start heading back — not urgently, but intentionally. You’ll have read the sky correctly.

This is the same judgment call experienced boaters on this river make every summer day. It becomes second nature quickly.

Heat and Shade

The hunting shoreline is fully exposed. Mid-summer sun on open sediment is hot, and it gets hotter through midday. The island’s interior, though, has genuine tree cover and is worth using for breaks — especially on longer trips. Step off the flats, get into the shade for ten minutes, drink water, then go back out. People who do this have better afternoons than people who grind through without stopping.

Sunblock, a hat, and more water than you think you’ll need are the standard summer kit. A lightweight long-sleeve shirt holds up better than sunblock alone on a full day out.

What’s Different on the Water

Boat traffic. Weekends from Memorial Day through Labor Day bring more recreational traffic on the Cape Fear than any other time of year. Midweek trips are noticeably quieter. If you have flexibility, Tuesday through Thursday mornings are as good as summer gets on this river.

Water temperature. The Cape Fear reaches 80–84°F by July. Warm water means dolphin activity is high, and the sharks whose teeth you’re hunting are actively in residence — sandbar, sand tiger, and bull sharks are all present in the lower river through summer. The same water that produced the fossil record is still producing it.

The flats themselves. Summer low tides, particularly around the new and full moon, expose significant material. Heat dries the upper sediment layer quickly once it’s out of the water, which can make color-spotting a little easier in the first few inches — dark teeth stand out against pale dried sand.
